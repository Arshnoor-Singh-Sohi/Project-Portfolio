## 1. The question this idea answers

Speech changes fast. Sounds start, stop and change many times in one second. To follow these changes, a voice system cuts the audio into short frames and takes one FFT for each frame (L34). How short can a frame be? A short frame shows when things happen. But L31 showed that a narrow signal has a wide spectrum. So a short frame blurs the frequencies.

Is this blur a weakness of the FFT, or a law of signals? This lesson shows that it is a law. You can choose the balance between time detail and frequency detail. You cannot have both at the same time. This choice sets the frame size of every spectrogram and of every speech front end.

## 2. Mental model

> [!analogy] A note heard for a moment
> Listen to a piano note for one second. You can name its pitch. Now hear only 5 ms of the same note. You hear a click. You know exactly when it happened, but not which note it was. A long listen gives the pitch. A short listen gives the time.
>
> **Where it stops working:** a trained ear uses tricks, such as context and memory, and the ear is not an FFT. The limit in this lesson is a property of the signal itself, not of a listener. No instrument and no algorithm can beat it.

## 3. Terms

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| window duration ($D$) | The length of one frame in seconds: $D = N\Delta t = N/f_s$. |
| frequency resolution | The smallest gap between two frequencies that the spectrum shows as two separate peaks. |
| time resolution | The shortest time detail that the spectrum can follow. It is about one window duration. |
| main lobe | The central peak of the spectrum of a window. Every tone in a frame appears with this shape (L31). |
| time spread ($\sigma_t$) | The standard deviation of $\lvert x(t) \rvert^2$, read as a distribution over time. |
| frequency spread ($\sigma_f$) | The standard deviation of $\lvert X(f) \rvert^2$, read as a distribution over frequency. |
| Gabor limit | $\sigma_t\, \sigma_f \ge \frac{1}{4\pi}$ for every signal. Only the Gaussian reaches it. |
| Cauchy–Schwarz inequality | A dot product is never larger than the product of the two lengths: $\lvert a \cdot b \rvert \le \lVert a \rVert\, \lVert b \rVert$. |
| fundamental frequency ($F_0$) | The repeat rate of the vocal-fold pulses in voiced speech. The harmonics sit at $F_0, 2F_0, 3F_0, \dots$ |
| formant | A broad peak in the spectrum of speech, made by a resonance of the vocal tract. |
| wideband spectrogram | A spectrogram with a short window (about 5 ms). It shows pulses and formants, but no single harmonics. |
| narrowband spectrogram | A spectrogram with a long window (about 30 ms). It shows single harmonics, but blurs fast changes. |

## 4. Bin spacing is one over the window duration

The bin spacing of L23 is $\Delta f = f_s/N$. Use $f_s = 1/\Delta t$:

$$
\Delta f = \frac{f_s}{N} = \frac{1}{N\,\Delta t} = \frac{1}{D}
$$

The sample rate cancels. Only the duration of the window counts.

| Window D | N at 16 kHz | N at 8 kHz | N at 48 kHz | Bin spacing $\Delta f = 1/D$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 ms | 80 | 40 | 240 | 200 Hz |
| 10 ms | 160 | 80 | 480 | 100 Hz |
| 25 ms | 400 | 200 | 1200 | 40 Hz |
| 30 ms | 480 | 240 | 1440 | 33.3 Hz |
| 100 ms | 1600 | 800 | 4800 | 10 Hz |
| 0.5 s (course: N = 64 at 128 Hz) | | | | 2 Hz |

> [!key] A 25 ms window gives 40 Hz bins
> $\Delta f = 1/0.025\ \text{s} = 40$ Hz, at every sample rate. A higher sample rate adds bins at higher frequencies. It does not make the bins narrower.

> [!note] Source
> The Whisper speech recognizer uses `SAMPLE_RATE = 16000`, `N_FFT = 400` and `HOP_LENGTH = 160` with a Hann window (file `whisper/audio.py` in the openai-whisper package, version 20250625). So each frame lasts 400/16,000 s = 25 ms, and its bins are 40 Hz apart. A new frame starts every 10 ms.

### 5.2 Zero padding does not help

Pad the 25 ms frame with zeros to 4096 points, then to 65,536 points. Both spectra still show one peak (at 1011.7 Hz and 1010.0 Hz). Zero padding gives more samples of the same DTFT (L30). The DTFT of a 25 ms frame has one peak here, so every padded FFT shows one peak.

### 5.3 Why: every tone becomes a copy of the window spectrum

The frame is the signal multiplied by the window. By the modulation pair (L31), a tone at $f_1$ in the frame becomes a copy of the window spectrum $W(f)$, moved to $f_1$. Two tones give two copies. By the scaling pair (L31), a window of duration $D$ has a main lobe with a width proportional to $1/D$. For a Hann window, the main lobe reaches its first zeros at $\pm 2/D$. When two copies are closer than about their width, they merge into one peak.

>

> | 40 Hz | 1 | 79 of 200 |
> | 60 Hz | 1.5 | 118 of 200 |
> | 80 Hz | 2 | 200 of 200 |
> | 120 Hz | 3 | 200 of 200 |
>
> A "clear peak" is a peak higher than half of the largest peak in the band. Close to the limit, the result depends on the phases of the two tones. From a gap of 2 bins ($2/D$), every trial showed two peaks.

## 6. The Gabor limit

### 6.1 Width as a spread

Section 5 used rough widths. To make the law exact, measure width as a spread, as in statistics.

Read $\lvert x(t) \rvert^2 / E$ as a distribution over time, where $E = \int \lvert x(t) \rvert^2\, dt$ is the energy. Its mean $\bar t$ is where the signal sits. Its standard deviation is the time spread:

$$
\sigma_t^2 = \frac{1}{E}\int (t - \bar t)^2\, \lvert x(t) \rvert^2\, dt
\qquad
\sigma_f^2 = \frac{1}{E}\int (f - \bar f)^2\, \lvert X(f) \rvert^2\, df
$$

Parseval's theorem (L31) makes the energy the same in both domains. So $E$ divides both.

$$
\sigma_t\, \sigma_f \;\ge\; \frac{1}{4\pi} \approx 0.0796
$$

> [!key] The Gabor limit
> For every signal, the time spread times the frequency spread is at least $\frac{1}{4\pi}$. A narrow signal must have a wide spectrum. Only the Gaussian reaches the limit exactly.

### 6.2 Why the limit holds

Take a real signal, centered at $\bar t = 0$, with energy $E = 1$. A shift in time does not change $\lvert X(f) \rvert$ (L31), so the centering changes no spread. A real signal has $\lvert X(-f) \rvert = \lvert X(f) \rvert$ (L24), so $\bar f = 0$. The proof has five steps.

1. **Integrate by parts.** The slope of $x^2$ is $2x\,x'$. So $\int t \cdot 2x\,x'\, dt = \left[t\,x^2\right]_{-\infty}^{\infty} - \int x^2\, dt = 0 - 1 = -1$. So $\int (t\,x)(x')\, dt = -\frac{1}{2}$.
2. **Use the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.** For two functions $a$ and $b$, $\left(\int a\,b\, dt\right)^2 \le \int a^2\, dt \cdot \int b^2\, dt$. With $a = t\,x$ and $b = x'$, step 1 gives $\frac{1}{4} \le \int t^2 x^2\, dt \cdot \int x'^2\, dt$.
3. **The first factor** is $\sigma_t^2$, by the definition with $E = 1$.
4. **The second factor** uses two pairs of L31. The slope pair gives $x' \leftrightarrow i 2\pi f\, X(f)$. Parseval gives $\int x'^2\, dt = \int 4\pi^2 f^2 \lvert X(f) \rvert^2\, df = 4\pi^2 \sigma_f^2$.
5. **Combine.** $\frac{1}{4} \le \sigma_t^2 \cdot 4\pi^2 \sigma_f^2$. Take the square root: $\frac{1}{2} \le 2\pi\, \sigma_t\, \sigma_f$. So $\sigma_t\, \sigma_f \ge \frac{1}{4\pi}$.

> [!note] Why the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality holds
> F4 wrote the dot product as $a \cdot b = \lVert a \rVert\, \lVert b \rVert \cos\theta$. Since $\lvert \cos\theta \rvert \le 1$, the dot product is never larger than the product of the lengths. An integral of a product is a dot product with very many coordinates (L28, section 4). So the same rule holds for functions.

**Why the Gaussian reaches the limit.** Equality in step 2 needs $\cos\theta = \pm 1$: the two functions must point the same way. So $x'(t)$ must be a constant times $t\,x(t)$. L29 showed that the Gaussian has exactly this property: $x'(t) = -2\pi t\, x(t)$ for $e^{-\pi t^2}$.

### 6.3 Numbers for common shapes

Python computed $\sigma_t$ from its definition, and $\sigma_f$ from $\int x'^2\, dt / (4\pi^2 E)$ (step 4). A direct FFT of $\lvert X(f) \rvert^2$ gives the same $\sigma_f$ for the Gaussian and the Hann shape.

| Shape | $\sigma_t$ | $\sigma_f$ | $\sigma_t\, \sigma_f$ | Times the limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian $e^{-\pi t^2}$ | 0.282095 | 0.282095 | 0.079577 | 1.0000 |
| Gaussian $e^{-\pi (t/0.02)^2}$ (t in s) | 5.642 ms | 14.105 Hz | 0.079577 | 1.0000 |
| Hann, $D$ = 1 s | 0.141448 | 0.577350 | 0.081665 | 1.0262 |
| triangle $1 - \lvert t \rvert$ | 0.316228 | 0.275664 | 0.087173 | 1.0954 |
| two-sided exponential $e^{-\lvert t \rvert}$ | 0.707107 | 0.159155 | 0.112540 | 1.4142 |
| rectangle, width 1 s | 0.288675 | no limit | no limit | — |

1. Every product is at least $\frac{1}{4\pi} = 0.079577$. Only the Gaussians reach it.
2. The Hann window is only 2.6% above the limit. By this measure, it is close to the best possible balance.
3. The rectangle has sharp edges. Its spectrum $\mathrm{sinc}^2$ falls only as $1/f^2$, so $f^2 \lvert X \rvert^2$ does not fall at all. Its frequency spread has no limit. If you stop the integral at 10 Hz, Python gives 1.01 Hz. Up to 100 Hz it gives 3.18 Hz, and up to 1000 Hz it gives 10.07 Hz.

> [!trap] Two different widths
> $\sigma_f$ is not the main-lobe width. Take a 25 ms Hann window. Its spreads are $\sigma_t$ = 3.536 ms and $\sigma_f$ = 23.09 Hz. But its main lobe reaches its first zeros at ±80 Hz. Different width measures differ by constant factors. Each product of a time width and a frequency width is a constant for one window shape. Compare widths only of the same kind.

## 7. The trade-off in speech

### 7.1 Two scales in a voice

Voiced speech, such as a vowel, has two kinds of structure:

1. **In time:** the vocal folds close once per period $1/F_0$. Each closure is a short pulse.
2. **In frequency:** the pulses repeat, so the spectrum has harmonics at $F_0, 2F_0, 3F_0, \dots$ (L28). The vocal tract shapes them with broad peaks, the formants.

A typical adult voice has $F_0$ from about 90 Hz to about 255 Hz. So the pitch period is between about 3.9 ms and 11 ms, and the harmonics are 90 to 255 Hz apart.

> [!note] Source
> Wikipedia, "Voice frequency", cites Baken (2000), *Clinical Measurement of Speech and Voice*. A typical adult male has a fundamental frequency from 90 to 155 Hz. A typical adult female has 165 to 255 Hz.

### 7.4 Frame size in a voice agent

1. **Speech recognition.** A common front end uses 25 ms frames with a new frame every 10 ms, as in Whisper (section 4). The bins are 40 Hz apart. The mel filterbank then merges neighboring bins into wider bands (L36).
2. **The spectrogram.** The window sets the frequency detail, and the hop sets the time step between frames (L34).
3. **Delay.** A system can compute a frame only after the last sample of the frame arrives. So a longer window adds delay to every decision that needs that frame (L42).

## 8. Code

Build these functions in a lab.

1. `bin_spacing(D)`: return the bin spacing in hertz for a window of `D` seconds.
2. `count_peaks(f1, f2, D, fs, window)`: make two cosines with amplitude 1 and phase 0. Take one frame with the window (`"rect"` or `"hann"`), and zero-pad it to 65,536 points. Count the peaks between $f_1 - 60$ Hz and $f_2 + 60$ Hz that are higher than half of the largest one.
3. `spread_product(x, dt)`: for samples `x` of a real, centered signal with time step `dt`, return $\sigma_t\, \sigma_f$. Compute $\sigma_f$ from $\Delta t$ · FFT (L29).

Acceptance tests:

- `bin_spacing(0.025)` returns 40.0, and `bin_spacing(0.005)` returns 200.0.
- `count_peaks(1000, 1020, 0.025, 16000, "hann")` returns 1. With `D = 0.1` it returns 2.
- `count_peaks(1000, 1080, 0.025, 16000, "hann")` returns 2.
- `spread_product` of $e^{-\pi t^2}$ (with `dt = 1/512` from −10 s to 10 s) is within 0.0001 of $1/(4\pi)$ = 0.0796.
- `spread_product` of a Hann shape is near 0.0817, and of a triangle near 0.0872.
- `spread_product` of a few random smooth shapes is never below 0.0795.

## 9. Common mistakes

1. **A higher sample rate gives finer bins.** The bin spacing is $1/D$. Only a longer window gives finer bins.
2. **Zero padding separates close peaks.** It gives a smoother picture of the same DTFT. Merged peaks stay merged.
3. **The limit belongs to the FFT.** It belongs to the signal. Any method that uses only a short stretch of signal faces it.
4. **The rectangle is the "sharpest" window.** It has the smallest $\sigma_t$ for its length. But its sharp edges give it a frequency spread with no limit.
5. **$\sigma_f$ equals the main-lobe width.** They are different measures of width. Compare only widths of the same kind.
6. **One window for every task.** Pitch needs a long window. Pulses and fast onsets need a short window.

## 10. One-minute explanation

The bin spacing of a frame is one over its duration: a 25 ms window gives 40 Hz bins, at any sample rate. Two tones closer than about the main-lobe width merge into one peak, and zero padding cannot split them. The reason is the window: each tone in a frame becomes a copy of the window spectrum, and a short window has a wide spectrum. The exact law is the Gabor limit: time spread times frequency spread is at least $1/(4\pi)$, and only the Gaussian reaches it. The proof is the dot-product rule $\lvert \cos\theta \rvert \le 1$ applied to $t\,x(t)$ and $x'(t)$. For speech, a 30 ms window separates the harmonics of the voice, and a 5 ms window separates its pulses and shows the formants. A speech front end usually takes 25 ms frames every 10 ms. A longer frame also means a longer wait before the frame is complete.
