## 1. The question this idea answers

L28 gave the spectrum of a signal that repeats. Most real signals do not repeat: a click, one spoken word, one heartbeat. What is the spectrum of a signal that happens only once?

The answer is the **Fourier transform**. The idea is simple: a signal that happens once is a periodic signal with an infinite period. So we make the period of a Fourier series longer and longer, and we watch the coefficients. The sums of L28 become integrals over all time and all frequencies. At the end of this lesson, the FFT computes these integrals for us.

## 2. Mental model

> [!analogy] Posts on the frequency axis
> The Fourier series puts one post at each harmonic $k/T$ on the frequency axis. The height of the post is the coefficient. Make the period twice as long, and the posts come twice as close. With an infinite period, the posts touch. They become a continuous wall: the spectrum $X(f)$.
>
> **Where it stops working:** the posts do not only come closer. Their heights also shrink, because each coefficient $c_k$ is proportional to $1/T$. With an infinite period, each post has a height of 0. So we multiply each height by $T$ before we take the limit. The result $X(f)$ is an amount per hertz (a density), not an amount at one frequency.

## 3. Terms

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| non-periodic signal | A signal that does not repeat, for example one pulse. |
| Fourier transform ($X(f)$) | $X(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi f t}\, dt$. The matching score of $x(t)$ with a complex probe at every frequency $f$. |
| inverse Fourier transform | $x(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(f)\, e^{i 2\pi f t}\, df$. It rebuilds the signal from $X(f)$. |
| spectral density | An amount per hertz. $X(f)$ is a density: the area under $\lvert X \rvert$ over a band of frequencies counts, not one value. |
| rectangular pulse | A signal that is 1 for $\lvert t \rvert < \tau/2$ and 0 outside. $\tau$ is the pulse width. |
| sinc function | $\mathrm{sinc}(u) = \frac{\sin(\pi u)}{\pi u}$, with $\mathrm{sinc}(0) = 1$. Its zeros are at $u = \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$ |
| Gaussian | The bell curve $e^{-\pi t^2}$. Its transform is $e^{-\pi f^2}$. |
| transform pair | A signal and its transform, written $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(f)$. |

Use the period from $-T/2$ to $T/2$. Inside this period, only the pulse is not zero. So the complex coefficient of L28 is:

$$
c_k = \frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2} x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi k t/T}\, dt = \frac{1}{T}\int_{-\tau/2}^{\tau/2} e^{-i 2\pi k t/T}\, dt = \frac{\tau}{T}\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{k\tau}{T}\right)
$$

Section 5 shows the last step. Here are the numbers, with $\tau$ = 1 s, rounded to five decimals:

| Period T | Spacing $1/T$ | $c_0$ | $c_1$ | $c_2$ | $T \cdot c_0$ | $T \cdot c_1$ | $T \cdot c_2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 s | 0.5 Hz | 0.5 | 0.31831 | 0 | 1 | 0.63662 | 0 |
| 4 s | 0.25 Hz | 0.25 | 0.22508 | 0.15915 | 1 | 0.90032 | 0.63662 |
| 8 s | 0.125 Hz | 0.125 | 0.12181 | 0.11254 | 1 | 0.97450 | 0.90032 |

Two things happen when $T$ doubles:

1. The harmonics $f_k = k/T$ come twice as close.
2. Each $c_k$ gets smaller. The pulse fills less of the period, so its average gets smaller. But $T \cdot c_k$ does not shrink.

The value $T \cdot c_k$ depends only on the frequency $f_k = k/T$: it is $\tau\,\mathrm{sinc}(f_k \tau)$. For example, $T \cdot c_1$ at $T$ = 2 s and $T \cdot c_2$ at $T$ = 4 s are both 0.63662, because both are at 0.5 Hz. So all the values sit on one curve.

### 4.2 The limit

Write $f_k = k/T$ for the frequency of harmonic $k$, and $\Delta f = 1/T$ for the spacing. Multiply the coefficient formula by $T$:

$$
T\, c_k = \int_{-T/2}^{T/2} x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi f_k t}\, dt
$$

When $T$ goes to infinity, the limits of the integral cover all time, and the points $f_k$ fill the whole frequency axis. The limit is a function of a continuous frequency $f$. This function is the Fourier transform:

$$
X(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi f t}\, dt
$$

Now rebuild the signal. Start from the series of L28 and use $\Delta f = 1/T$:

$$
x(t) = \sum_k c_k\, e^{i 2\pi f_k t} = \sum_k (T c_k)\, e^{i 2\pi f_k t}\, \frac{1}{T} = \sum_k (T c_k)\, e^{i 2\pi f_k t}\, \Delta f
$$

The last sum is a strip sum over frequency: height $(T c_k)\,e^{i2\pi f_k t}$, width $\Delta f$. When $\Delta f$ goes to 0, it becomes an integral. This is the inverse Fourier transform:

$$
x(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(f)\, e^{i 2\pi f t}\, df
$$

> [!key] X(f) is a density
> $c_k$ is an amount at one frequency. $X(f) \approx T c_k$ is that amount divided by the spacing $\Delta f$. So $X(f)$ is an amount per hertz. If $x(t)$ is in volts, $X(f)$ is in volts per hertz (volt-seconds).

### 4.3 The same structure as the DFT

| | DFT (L11, L13) | Fourier transform |
|---|---|---|
| Time | samples $x[n]$, $n = 0 \dots N-1$ | continuous $x(t)$, all $t$ |
| Frequency | bins $k = 0 \dots N-1$ | continuous $f$, all $f$ |
| Forward | $X[k] = \sum_n x[n]\, e^{-i 2\pi k n/N}$ | $X(f) = \int x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi f t}\, dt$ |
| Inverse | $x[n] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_k X[k]\, e^{i 2\pi k n/N}$ | $x(t) = \int X(f)\, e^{i 2\pi f t}\, df$ |
| Probe | $e^{-i 2\pi k n/N}$ | $e^{-i 2\pi f t}$ |

The forward direction uses $e^{-i\dots}$ and the inverse uses $e^{+i\dots}$ in both. The $1/N$ of the inverse DFT has no copy in the transform. With $f$ in hertz, the width $df$ does its job.

> [!note] Other conventions
> Some books use the angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$. Then a factor $1/(2\pi)$ appears in the inverse. These notes always use $f$ in hertz, so no factor appears.

## 5. Example 1: the rectangular pulse gives a sinc

Take one pulse of width $\tau$: $x(t) = 1$ for $\lvert t \rvert < \tau/2$, and 0 outside. The integral covers only the pulse.

1. $X(f) = \int_{-\tau/2}^{\tau/2} e^{-i 2\pi f t}\, dt$.
2. The rule $\int e^{at}\, dt = \frac{e^{at}}{a}$ with $a = -i 2\pi f$ gives $X(f) = \frac{e^{-i\pi f\tau} - e^{i\pi f\tau}}{-i 2\pi f}$.
3. Change the sign of the top and the bottom: $X(f) = \frac{e^{i\pi f\tau} - e^{-i\pi f\tau}}{i 2\pi f}$.
4. Euler's formula gives $e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta} = 2i\sin\theta$. So $X(f) = \frac{2i \sin(\pi f\tau)}{i 2\pi f} = \frac{\sin(\pi f\tau)}{\pi f}$.
5. Multiply the top and the bottom by $\tau$: $X(f) = \tau\,\frac{\sin(\pi f\tau)}{\pi f\tau} = \tau\,\mathrm{sinc}(f\tau)$.

$$
\text{rectangle of width } \tau \;\leftrightarrow\; \tau\,\mathrm{sinc}(f\tau) = \frac{\sin(\pi f \tau)}{\pi f}
$$

Read the result:

1. **The value at 0 Hz is the area.** $X(0) = \tau$, the area of the pulse. In general $X(0) = \int x(t)\,dt$, because $e^{0} = 1$.
2. **The zeros are at multiples of $1/\tau$.** $\sin(\pi f\tau) = 0$ when $f\tau$ is a whole number $m \ne 0$. So the zeros are at $f = m/\tau$. At these frequencies the probe makes exactly $m$ cycles over the pulse, and the matching score cancels (L8).
3. **Negative values are a phase of 180°.** The pulse is symmetric around $t = 0$, so $X(f)$ is real. A negative value is a real number with phase $\pi$.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{sinc}(f)$ for $\tau$ = 1 s | 1 | 0.9003 | 0.6366 | 0 | −0.2122 | 0 | 0.1273 |

## 6. Example 2: the Gaussian gives a Gaussian

Take $x(t) = e^{-\pi t^2}$. The steps below show that $X(f) = e^{-\pi f^2}$: the same curve.

1. **The slope of the signal.** The derivative is $x'(t) = -2\pi t\, e^{-\pi t^2} = -2\pi t\, x(t)$.
2. **The slope of the transform.** Take the derivative of $X(f)$ with respect to $f$, inside the integral: $\frac{dX}{df} = \int x(t)\,(-i 2\pi t)\, e^{-i 2\pi f t}\, dt = i\int x'(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi f t}\, dt$. The last step uses step 1.
3. **Integrate by parts.** $\int x'(t)\, e^{-i2\pi f t}\, dt = \left[x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi f t}\right]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + i 2\pi f \int x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi f t}\, dt$. The bracket is 0, because $x(t)$ goes to 0 at both ends. So the integral is $i 2\pi f\, X(f)$.
4. **Combine.** $\frac{dX}{df} = i \cdot i 2\pi f\, X(f) = -2\pi f\, X(f)$.
5. **The start value.** $X(0) = \int e^{-\pi t^2}\, dt = 1$ (see the note below).
6. **Solve.** The function $e^{-\pi f^2}$ has slope $-2\pi f\, e^{-\pi f^2}$ and the value 1 at $f = 0$. It obeys the same equation with the same start value. Such an equation has only one solution. So $X(f) = e^{-\pi f^2}$.

The signal and its transform obey the same equation: "slope = $-2\pi$ × position × value". That is why they have the same shape.

> [!note] Why the area under $e^{-\pi t^2}$ is 1
> Call the area $I$. Then $I^2 = \int\int e^{-\pi(x^2 + y^2)}\, dx\, dy$, an integral over the whole plane. Use polar coordinates: $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$, and a thin ring of radius $r$ has area $2\pi r\, dr$. So $I^2 = \int_0^\infty e^{-\pi r^2}\, 2\pi r\, dr = \left[-e^{-\pi r^2}\right]_0^\infty = 1$. So $I = 1$.

> [!example] Numbers
> $X(0) = 1$. $X(0.5) = e^{-\pi/4} = 0.455938$. $X(1) = e^{-\pi} = 0.043214$. $X(1.5) = 0.000851$. A numeric integral of $e^{-\pi t^2}\cos(2\pi f t)$ in Python gives the same six decimals. The sine part is 0, because the Gaussian is even.

## 7. Compute the transform with the FFT

### 7.1 From the integral to a DFT

A computer cannot integrate over all time. But section 4 of L28 showed what to do: replace the integral with a strip sum.

1. Sample $x(t)$ with time step $\Delta t$ at $t_n = n\Delta t$, for $n = -N/2 \dots N/2 - 1$. The samples cover a span of $N\Delta t$ seconds.
2. Replace the integral with a strip sum: $X(f) \approx \sum_n x(t_n)\, e^{-i 2\pi f t_n}\, \Delta t$.
3. Use only the frequencies $f_k = \frac{k}{N\Delta t} = \frac{k f_s}{N}$. These are the bin frequencies of L23.
4. Then $f_k t_n = \frac{k}{N\Delta t} \cdot n\Delta t = \frac{kn}{N}$. The sum becomes $\Delta t \sum_n x(t_n)\, e^{-i 2\pi k n/N}$: a DFT times $\Delta t$.

$$
X(f_k) \approx \Delta t \cdot \mathrm{DFT}\{x(t_n)\}[k], \qquad f_k = \frac{k}{N\Delta t}
$$

One detail remains. The DFT wants the samples in the order $n = 0 \dots N-1$. Our samples run from $n = -N/2$. The DFT treats its input as one period (L30), so index $n$ and index $n + N$ are the same sample. So the negative times go to the end of the array. `np.fft.ifftshift` does this move. It puts the sample at $t = 0$ at index 0.

```python
X = dt * np.fft.fft(np.fft.ifftshift(x))
f = np.fft.fftfreq(N, dt)
```

Now use a coarse step: $N$ = 128 and $\Delta t$ = 1/8 s. The span is still 16 s, but the sample rate is only 8 Hz. The Gaussian error stays near $2 \times 10^{-16}$. The rectangle error grows to 0.066. At 1.5 Hz the FFT gives −0.18708 instead of −0.21221.

The reason is aliasing (L26). The rectangle has sharp edges, so its spectrum never ends. The parts above $f_s/2$ = 4 Hz fold onto lower frequencies. The Gaussian spectrum is almost 0 above 2 Hz: $e^{-\pi \cdot 4} \approx 3.5 \times 10^{-6}$ at 2 Hz, and much less above. So almost nothing folds.

> [!key] When Δt · FFT is a good transform
> 1. The time step is small enough: $x(t)$ has almost no content above $f_s/2$.
> 2. The span $N\Delta t$ covers all of $x(t)$. Otherwise the cut edges add leakage (L25).
> 3. You multiply by $\Delta t$, and you put $t = 0$ at index 0.

> [!trap] No ifftshift
> If you give the FFT the samples from −8 s in natural order, it treats the first sample as $t = 0$. That is a shift of 8 s. The values alternate in sign: 1, −0.9878, 0.9521, −0.8954, and so on. The magnitudes are correct, but each value has an extra factor $e^{-i 2\pi f_k t_0} = (-1)^k$, with $t_0 = -8$ s. L31 explains this factor: a time shift rotates the phase.

## 9. Common mistakes

1. **No $\Delta t$.** The FFT gives the strip sum without the strip width. Multiply by $\Delta t$ to get $X(f)$. Without it, the values are $1/\Delta t$ times too big.
2. **The wrong time origin.** The FFT treats index 0 as $t = 0$. Use `np.fft.ifftshift`, or correct the phase.
3. **$X(f)$ read as an amplitude.** $X(f)$ is a density, in units per hertz. A Fourier series coefficient $c_k$ is an amplitude.
4. **The wrong sinc.** `np.sinc(u)` is $\frac{\sin(\pi u)}{\pi u}$. It is not $\frac{\sin u}{u}$.
5. **A short span.** If the span $N\Delta t$ is shorter than the signal, the result is the transform of a cut signal. Make the span longer than the signal.
6. **A large time step for a sharp signal.** Sharp edges have content at all frequencies. A large $\Delta t$ folds this content onto low frequencies.

## 10. One-minute explanation

A signal that happens only once is a periodic signal with an infinite period. In a Fourier series, the harmonics are $1/T$ apart. When $T$ grows, they come closer and become a continuous frequency axis. Each coefficient shrinks like $1/T$, so I multiply it by $T$. The limit is the Fourier transform $X(f)$: the matching score of the signal with the complex probe at every frequency. It is a density, an amount per hertz. A rectangle of width $\tau$ gives $\tau\,\mathrm{sinc}(f\tau)$, with zeros at multiples of $1/\tau$. A Gaussian gives the same Gaussian. To compute a transform, I sample the signal finely, put $t = 0$ at index 0, run the FFT and multiply by $\Delta t$.

  "answer": "$X(0) = \\int x(t)\\,dt$, the area under the signal. The triangle has base 2 and height 1, so its area is $\\frac{1}{2} \\times 2 \\times 1 = 1$. So $X(0) = 1$."
