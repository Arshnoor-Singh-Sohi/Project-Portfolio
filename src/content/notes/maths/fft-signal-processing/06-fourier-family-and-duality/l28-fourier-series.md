## 1. The question this idea answers

Part 05 ended with the spectrum of a list of N samples. But a real sound also exists between the samples. A vibrating string or a vocal fold makes a continuous signal $x(t)$ that repeats. Which frequencies does a continuous, periodic signal contain, and how much of each?

The DFT (L11) answers this question for a finite list of samples. This lesson answers it for a continuous signal that repeats with period $T$. The answer is the **Fourier series**. It uses the matching score of L7, with an integral in place of a sum.

The story so far:

1. L7 to L13: the matching score, orthogonal probes, the DFT and the inverse DFT. All are finite sums.
2. Part 04: the FFT computes the same sums fast.
3. Part 05: bins become hertz, and the spectrum has limits (leakage, aliasing).
4. Part 06 (this part): the continuous members of the Fourier family. Every continuous formula here is the limit of a DFT-style sum.

## 2. Mental model

> [!analogy] A looped bar of music
> A music producer loops one bar that lasts $T$ seconds. A note repeats cleanly in the loop only if it makes a whole number of cycles in one bar. So the loop can hold only the frequencies $1/T$, $2/T$, $3/T$, and so on. The Fourier series lists how loud each allowed note is, and where each note starts (its phase).
>
> **Where it stops working:** a real note has a start and an end. A harmonic in a Fourier series never starts and never stops. It runs with the same amplitude for all time. Also, a sharp jump in the loop needs an infinite number of notes. Any finite set of notes overshoots near the jump.

## 3. Terms

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| periodic signal | A signal with $x(t + T) = x(t)$ for every $t$. It repeats with period $T$. |
| fundamental frequency ($f_0$) | $f_0 = 1/T$. The frequency that makes exactly one cycle in one period. |
| angular frequency ($\omega$) | $\omega = 2\pi f_0$, in radians per second. Then $\sin(\omega t)$ makes one cycle in $T$. |
| harmonic | A sine or a cosine at a whole-number multiple $k f_0$. Harmonic $k$ makes $k$ cycles in one period. |
| integral | The limit of a sum of thin strips (height × width) as the width goes to 0. It is the signed area under a curve. |
| Fourier series | A periodic signal written as a constant plus a sum of harmonics. |
| Fourier coefficient | The amount of one harmonic: $a_k$, $b_k$ in the real form, $c_k$ in the complex form. |
| constant term ($a_0$) | The average value of $x(t)$ over one period. Engineers also call it the DC term. |
| partial sum ($S_M$) | The series stopped after $M$ terms. |
| square wave | A signal that is $+1$ for the first half of each period and $-1$ for the second half. |
| Gibbs phenomenon | The overshoot of a partial sum next to a jump. As $M$ grows, it approaches about 9% of the jump. It does not go to 0. |
| band-limited signal | A signal with no harmonics above some highest frequency. |

## 4. From the matching score to an integral

### 4.1 The sum needs a width

L7 defined the matching score of a signal $x$ and a probe $p$: multiply point by point, then add.

$$
s = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\, p[n]
$$

Take $N$ samples of one period, so the time step is $\Delta t = T/N$. If you double $N$, you add twice as many terms, and $s$ roughly doubles. A score that changes with $N$ cannot describe the continuous signal. So multiply each term by the width $\Delta t$:

$$
s_{\Delta t} = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x(n\Delta t)\, p(n\Delta t)\, \Delta t
$$

Each term is now a thin strip: a height $x(n\Delta t)\,p(n\Delta t)$ times a width $\Delta t$. When $\Delta t$ goes to 0, the strips fill the area under the curve $x(t)\,p(t)$. This limit is the integral:

$$
\int_0^T x(t)\, p(t)\, dt = \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x(n\Delta t)\, p(n\Delta t)\, \Delta t
$$

> [!key] An integral is the limit of a sum
> Read $\int_0^T \dots dt$ as "add value × $\Delta t$ over one period, with a very small $\Delta t$". Every continuous formula in this part is a DFT-style sum with $\Delta t$ made very small.

### 4.2 A worked example with real numbers

> [!example] The score of $\sin(2\pi t)$ with itself, $T$ = 1 s
> Use $x(t) = p(t) = \sin(2\pi t)$. The product is $\sin^2(2\pi t)$.
>
> | N | $\Delta t$ | Samples of $\sin^2(2\pi n/N)$ | Sum of samples | Sum × $\Delta t$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 2 | 1/2 | 0, 0 | 0 | 0 |
> | 4 | 1/4 | 0, 1, 0, 1 | 2 | 0.5 |
> | 8 | 1/8 | 0, 0.5, 1, 0.5, 0, 0.5, 1, 0.5 | 4 | 0.5 |

> The integral is exactly $T/2 = 0.5$. With 8 samples the sum already gives 0.5 exactly. With 2 samples it gives 0, because both samples fall on zeros of the sine (aliasing, L26).

## 5. Harmonics and orthogonality

### 5.1 Which probes fit a period

A probe must repeat with the same period $T$ as the signal. Otherwise its score changes from one period to the next. The probes $\cos(k\omega t)$ and $\sin(k\omega t)$ with a whole number $k$ make exactly $k$ cycles in $T$. So they fit. Here $\omega = 2\pi/T$.

These probes are the harmonics. Their frequencies are $f_0, 2f_0, 3f_0, \dots$ with $f_0 = 1/T$. A periodic signal cannot contain any other frequency. A frequency of $1.5 f_0$, for example, does not repeat after one period.

### 5.2 The three orthogonality integrals

For whole numbers $j, k \ge 0$, over one period:

$$
\int_0^T \cos(j\omega t)\cos(k\omega t)\, dt = \begin{cases} 0 & j \ne k \\ T/2 & j = k \ge 1 \\ T & j = k = 0 \end{cases}
$$

$$
\int_0^T \sin(j\omega t)\sin(k\omega t)\, dt = \begin{cases} 0 & j \ne k \\ T/2 & j = k \ge 1 \end{cases}
\qquad
\int_0^T \cos(j\omega t)\sin(k\omega t)\, dt = 0
$$

These are the continuous versions of the orthogonality of L9. Here is the proof of the first one, step by step.

1. Use the product rule from trigonometry: $\cos A \cos B = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)\right]$.
2. With $A = j\omega t$ and $B = k\omega t$, the product becomes $\tfrac{1}{2}\cos((j-k)\omega t) + \tfrac{1}{2}\cos((j+k)\omega t)$.
3. A cosine with $m \ne 0$ whole cycles in $T$ has an integral of 0 over one period. Its area above zero equals its area below zero. Exactly: $\int_0^T \cos(m\omega t)\,dt = \frac{\sin(m\omega T)}{m\omega} - 0 = \frac{\sin(2\pi m)}{m\omega} = 0$.
4. If $j \ne k$, both terms have $m \ne 0$. So the integral is 0.
5. If $j = k \ge 1$, the first term is $\tfrac{1}{2}\cos 0 = \tfrac{1}{2}$. Its integral is $T/2$. The second term gives 0.

> [!note] One rule from calculus
> Step 3 uses the rule $\int \cos(m\omega t)\, dt = \frac{\sin(m\omega t)}{m\omega}$. The reason: the slope (derivative) of $\sin(m\omega t)$ is $m\omega \cos(m\omega t)$. The sine-times-sine and the cosine-times-sine integrals use the same steps.

A numeric check: the 8-sample sum of $\sin(2\pi t)\sin(6\pi t) \cdot \tfrac{1}{8}$ is $9.7 \times 10^{-17}$. This is 0 up to rounding.

### 5.3 The series and its coefficients

The real form of the Fourier series is:

$$
x(t) = a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left[ a_k \cos(k\omega t) + b_k \sin(k\omega t) \right]
$$

To get $b_j$, use the matching score with the probe $\sin(j\omega t)$:

1. Multiply both sides by $\sin(j\omega t)$.
2. Integrate both sides over one period. Integrate the sum term by term. This step is valid for all signals in this subject.
3. Orthogonality makes every term 0, except the term $b_j \sin(j\omega t)\sin(j\omega t)$. Its integral is $b_j\, T/2$.
4. So $\int_0^T x(t)\sin(j\omega t)\, dt = b_j\, T/2$.

$$
b_k = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\sin(k\omega t)\, dt
\qquad
a_k = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\cos(k\omega t)\, dt
\qquad
a_0 = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\, dt
$$

The factor is $2/T$ because the score of a harmonic with itself is $T/2$. The constant term uses $1/T$, because the score of 1 with itself is $T$.

> [!key] Orthogonality makes each coefficient independent
> The score with probe $j$ ignores every other harmonic. So you get each coefficient from one integral, with no system of equations. If you add more harmonics to the series, the coefficients you already have do not change.

## 6. Worked example: the square wave

The square wave is $x(t) = +1$ for $0 < t < T/2$ and $x(t) = -1$ for $T/2 < t < T$.

**The constant term and the cosines.** The average over one period is 0, so $a_0 = 0$. Use the period from $-T/2$ to $T/2$. The square wave is odd: $x(-t) = -x(t)$. A cosine is even. Their product is odd, so its area on the left cancels its area on the right. So $a_k = 0$ for every $k$.

**The sines.** Split the integral at $T/2$. Use $\int \sin(k\omega t)\, dt = -\frac{\cos(k\omega t)}{k\omega}$ and $k\omega \cdot \frac{T}{2} = k\pi$.

1. First half: $\int_0^{T/2} \sin(k\omega t)\, dt = \frac{1 - \cos(k\pi)}{k\omega}$.
2. Second half: $\int_{T/2}^{T} \sin(k\omega t)\, dt = \frac{\cos(k\pi) - \cos(2k\pi)}{k\omega} = \frac{\cos(k\pi) - 1}{k\omega}$.
3. The signal is $+1$ in the first half and $-1$ in the second half. So the score is the first result minus the second: $\frac{2(1 - \cos k\pi)}{k\omega}$.
4. Multiply by $2/T$, with $\omega T = 2\pi$: $b_k = \frac{4(1 - \cos k\pi)}{k\omega T} = \frac{2}{\pi k}\left(1 - (-1)^k\right)$.

For odd $k$, $(-1)^k = -1$, so $b_k = \frac{4}{\pi k}$. For even $k$, $b_k = 0$.

$$
x(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\left( \sin \omega t + \frac{\sin 3\omega t}{3} + \frac{\sin 5\omega t}{5} + \dots \right)
$$

| k | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 7 | 9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $b_k$ exact | $4/\pi$ | 0 | $4/(3\pi)$ | 0 | $4/(5\pi)$ | $4/(7\pi)$ | $4/(9\pi)$ |
| $b_k$ rounded to four decimals | 1.2732 | 0 | 0.4244 | 0 | 0.2546 | 0.1819 | 0.1415 |

### 7.2 Why the height stays: a sum becomes an integral

Write the partial sum with the points $u_j = (2j - 1)\omega t$ for $j = 1 \dots M$. Neighboring points are $\Delta u = 2\omega t$ apart.

1. Each term is $\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{\sin u_j}{2j - 1}$. Since $2j - 1 = u_j/(\omega t)$, the term equals $\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{\sin u_j}{u_j}\,\omega t$.
2. Replace $\omega t$ with $\Delta u / 2$. Then $S_M(t) = \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{M} \frac{\sin u_j}{u_j}\,\Delta u$.
3. This is a sum of strips under the curve $\frac{\sin u}{u}$, from $u = 0$ to $U = 2M\omega t$.
4. With many terms, the strips are thin. So $S_M(t)$ approaches $\frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{U} \frac{\sin u}{u}\, du$.

The integral $\mathrm{Si}(U) = \int_0^U \frac{\sin u}{u}\,du$ is the sine integral. It has its maximum at $U = \pi$, the first zero of $\frac{\sin u}{u}$. A computer gives $\mathrm{Si}(\pi) = 1.851937$. So:

$$
\text{peak} \to \frac{2}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Si}(\pi) = 1.178980
\qquad
\text{overshoot} = 0.178980 = 0.08949 \times \text{jump}
$$

The peak sits at $U = \pi$, so $2M\omega t = \pi$ and $t = \frac{\pi}{2M\omega} = \frac{T}{4M}$. This matches the table.

> [!key] The overshoot gets narrower, not lower
> The shape near the jump depends only on $U = 2M\omega t$. More terms squeeze the same shape closer to the jump. The height stays at about 9% of the jump. The area of the error goes to 0, so the average error goes to 0.

> [!trap] At the jump itself
> At $t = 0$ every sine is 0, so $S_M(0) = 0$ for every $M$. The series gives the midpoint of the jump, not $+1$ or $-1$. When you sample a jump, use the midpoint value for the sample on the jump.

Gibbs ringing is not only a property of the square wave. It appears next to any jump, when a sum stops at a highest frequency. Lesson L31 shows the same 9% ripple when a rectangle appears in the frequency domain.

## 8. The complex form

Euler's formula (F6) writes each cosine and sine as two rotations:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2}
\qquad
\sin\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i}
$$

Put these into the real form and collect the terms with the same $e^{ik\omega t}$. The result is the complex form:

$$
x(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} c_k\, e^{i 2\pi k t / T}
\qquad
c_k = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi k t / T}\, dt
$$

The coefficients of the two forms connect like this:

$$
c_0 = a_0 \qquad c_k = \frac{a_k - i\, b_k}{2} \qquad c_{-k} = \frac{a_k + i\, b_k}{2} = \overline{c_k} \quad (k \ge 1, \text{ real } x)
$$

The formula for $c_k$ uses the same steps as $b_k$. The complex probes are orthogonal over one period:

$$
\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T e^{i 2\pi (j - k) t/T}\, dt = \begin{cases} 1 & j = k \\ 0 & j \ne k \end{cases}
$$

This is the continuous version of the sum in L10: $\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} e^{i 2\pi (j-k) n/N}$ is $N$ or 0.

> [!example] The square wave in complex form
> $a_k = 0$ and $b_k = \frac{4}{\pi k}$ for odd $k$. So $c_k = \frac{-i\, b_k}{2} = \frac{-2i}{\pi k}$ for odd $k \ge 1$, and $c_{-k} = \frac{+2i}{\pi k}$.
>
> - $c_1 = -0.6366i$ and $c_{-1} = +0.6366i$.
> - $c_3 = -0.2122i$ and $c_{-3} = +0.2122i$.
> - $c_0 = 0$, and $c_k = 0$ for every even $k$.
>
> A numeric integral in Python gives the same values to six decimals.

> [!note] Names
> Many books write $c_n$. These notes write $c_k$, because $k$ counts frequencies and $n$ counts samples, as in the DFT.

## 9. The link to the DFT

### 9.1 The DFT is a strip sum for $c_k$

Put the two formulas side by side:

$$
c_k = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi k t/T}\, dt
\qquad
\frac{X[k]}{N} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\, e^{-i 2\pi k n/N}
$$

Sample one period: $t = n\Delta t$ with $\Delta t = T/N$, and $x[n] = x(n\Delta t)$. Replace the integral with its strip sum:

$$
c_k \approx \frac{1}{T}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x(n\Delta t)\, e^{-i 2\pi k n \Delta t/T}\, \Delta t = \frac{1}{T}\cdot\frac{T}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\, e^{-i 2\pi k n/N} = \frac{X[k]}{N}
$$

So $X[k]/N$ is the strip sum for $c_k$. When is the strip sum exact?

### 9.2 Exact for a band-limited signal

Put the series into the DFT. The sample value is $x[n] = \sum_j c_j e^{i 2\pi j n/N}$. Then:

$$
X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_j c_j\, e^{i 2\pi (j - k) n/N} = N \sum_{m} c_{k + mN}
$$

The inner sum over $n$ is $N$ when $j - k$ is a multiple of $N$, and 0 otherwise (L10). So $X[k]$ collects $c_k$ and every $c_{k + mN}$, for whole numbers $m$.

> [!key] The DFT of one period gives N times the Fourier coefficients
> If $x(t)$ has no harmonics with $\lvert k \rvert \ge N/2$, only one term remains. Then $X[k] = N c_k$ for $0 \le k < N/2$, and $X[N - k] = N c_{-k}$. If the signal has higher harmonics, they fold onto lower bins. This folding is aliasing (L26).

> [!example] The teaching signal is one period of a band-limited signal
> Take $x(t) = \cos(2\pi t/T) + 0.5\sin(2\pi \cdot 2t/T)$. Its coefficients are $c_1 = c_{-1} = 0.5$, $c_2 = 0.5/(2i) = -0.25i$ and $c_{-2} = +0.25i$. All others are 0.
>
> Eight samples of one period give the teaching signal $x[n] = \cos(2\pi n/8) + 0.5\sin(2\pi\cdot 2n/8)$. Its DFT (L11) is $X[1] = 4$, $X[2] = -2i$, $X[6] = 2i$, $X[7] = 4$.
>
> - $X[1] = 8 \times 0.5 = 4$ and $X[7] = 8\, c_{-1} = 4$.
> - $X[2] = 8 \times (-0.25i) = -2i$ and $X[6] = 8\, c_{-2} = 2i$.
>
> The match is exact.

> [!example] The square wave is not band-limited
> Eight samples of one period, with the midpoint 0 on each jump: $[0, 1, 1, 1, 0, -1, -1, -1]$. By hand, with $e^{-i\pi/4} = 0.7071 - 0.7071i$:
>
> - The real parts cancel: $0.7071 + 0 - 0.7071 + 0.7071 + 0 - 0.7071 = 0$.
> - The imaginary parts add: $-(0.7071 + 1 + 0.7071) - (0.7071 + 1 + 0.7071) = -4.8284$.
>
> So $X[1] = -4.8284i$ (exactly $-(2 + 2\sqrt{2})\,i$), and $X[1]/8 = -0.6036i$. The true $c_1$ is $-0.6366i$. The error 0.0331 is the sum of the folded coefficients $c_9 + c_{-7} + c_{17} + \dots$. A Python sum of $c_1$ and these terms gives $-0.6036i$. With 64 samples, $X[1]/64 = -0.6361i$, and the error drops to 0.0005.

This page rounds the decimals, for example $1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.7071$. The course rounds the same value to 0.71.

## 11. Common mistakes

1. **An integral over less than one full period.** Orthogonality needs a whole number of cycles of every harmonic. Integrate over exactly one period $T$.
2. **The wrong factor.** $a_k$ and $b_k$ use $2/T$. The constant term $a_0$ and the complex $c_k$ use $1/T$.
3. **"More terms remove the overshoot."** More terms make the overshoot narrower. Its height stays about 9% of the jump.
4. **A frequency between harmonics.** A signal with period $T$ contains only $k/T$. A component at $1.5/T$ would break the period.
5. **$X[k]$ read as $c_k$.** With the DFT of this subject, $X[k] = N c_k$. Divide by $N$ first.
6. **The DFT is always exact.** It is exact only for a band-limited signal with no harmonics at $\lvert k \rvert \ge N/2$. Higher harmonics fold onto lower bins.

## 12. One-minute explanation

A signal that repeats every $T$ seconds can contain only frequencies that fit the repeat: $1/T$, $2/T$, $3/T$, and so on. These are the harmonics. To measure one harmonic, I use the matching score from the DFT, but with an integral. I multiply the signal by the harmonic. Then I add value times $\Delta t$ over one period, with a very small $\Delta t$. The harmonics are orthogonal, so each score sees only its own harmonic. For a square wave, only the odd sines appear, with sizes $4/(\pi k)$. A finite sum always overshoots next to a jump by about 9% of the jump. More terms make the overshoot narrower, not lower. If I take $N$ samples of one period of a band-limited signal, the DFT gives exactly $N$ times the coefficients.
